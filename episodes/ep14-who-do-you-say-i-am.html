Did Jesus really exist?

Yes. Nearly all historians, Christian or not, agree Jesus of Nazareth existed, and Roman and Jewish writers such as Tacitus and Josephus mention him. Whether he rose from the dead is the bigger question, and the episode weighs the empty tomb and the disciples' willingness to suffer.

From Episode 14: Who Do You Say I Am?. Host Daniel Voss with Dr. Ruth Calloway, historian of the ancient Mediterranean and early Christianity.

Jesus Never Existed

Daniel: The title comes from Matthew sixteen. Jesus turns to his disciples and asks, "Who do people say that I am?" They give him the rumors. And then he asks the real question. "But who do YOU say that I am?" Ruth, before we get to who he was — was he? Did he exist?

Ruth: You want the short answer or the honest one?

Daniel: Both.

Ruth: Short answer: yes. Honest answer: yes, and it's not even close. I've spent thirty-five years in this field, and I can count on one hand the credentialed historians of antiquity who argue Jesus never existed. It's a fringe view. Bart Ehrman — who is NOT a Christian, he's an agnostic, and he's made a career out of challenging the faith — wrote a whole book in 2012 called "Did Jesus Exist?" Just to answer that question. And his answer is yes. Emphatically.

Daniel: So my TA —

Ruth: — your TA had it backwards. Bless his heart.

Daniel: Okay. So what's the evidence outside the Bible? Because that's what people always ask for.

Ruth: Let me start with my favorite, because he can't stand Christians. Tacitus. Roman senator, probably the greatest Roman historian. Around the year 116, he's writing about the great fire of Rome under Nero. And he explains who these "Christians" are that Nero blamed for it. He says — I'm paraphrasing his Latin — their name comes from Christus, who was executed by the procurator Pontius Pilate during the reign of Tiberius. And then he calls the whole movement "a most mischievous superstition."

Daniel: So he's not a fan.

Ruth: He is NOT a fan. Which is exactly why historians love him. A hostile witness. He's got no reason to help you out.

Daniel: And Josephus.

Ruth: Josephus is the Jewish historian, writing in the 90s. He mentions Jesus twice. One passage — scholars call it the Testimonium — is a little complicated, because later Christian copyists clearly added some lines to it. Things like "he was the Christ." A Jewish writer in Rome wouldn't have said that.

Daniel: So it's been tampered with.

Ruth: Embellished. But most scholars think there's a real core underneath — that Josephus did write about a wise man named Jesus who was crucified under Pilate. And the second mention is clean. Book twenty. He's describing how a man named James was stoned in Jerusalem in the year 62. And he identifies him as — quote — "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ."

Daniel: Just a passing reference.

Ruth: That's what makes it gold. It's a throwaway line. Nobody forges a throwaway line.

Daniel: Now, you'll hear people add Pliny the Younger to the list —

Ruth: — and I'm going to be a little picky about Pliny, if you don't mind.

Daniel: Go ahead.

Ruth: Pliny was a Roman governor in what's now Turkey, around 112. He writes the emperor Trajan asking what to do with all these Christians. And he says they meet before dawn and sing hymns "to Christ as to a god." Now, that doesn't tell us anything about Jesus's life. What it tells us is that within about eighty years, ordinary people in a Roman province were worshipping him. That's a different kind of evidence. It's still remarkable. But I wouldn't stand up and say "Pliny proves Jesus existed."

Daniel: That's fair. I'll take the correction. And honestly, the earliest witness isn't Roman at all. It's Paul. Galatians one — Paul says he went up to Jerusalem and met Peter, and "James, the Lord's brother."

Ruth: Right. Paul is writing in the 50s. He personally knew Jesus's brother. You don't get to be a brother of someone who never existed.

Daniel: That's going on a t-shirt.

Ruth: And it's not only writers. The ground keeps talking, too. For nearly two thousand years, everything we knew about Pontius Pilate came from texts. Then in 1961, Italian archaeologists digging at Caesarea, on the coast of Israel, turned over a block of limestone — it had been reused as a step in a theater — and there's his name carved into it. Pontius Pilatus, prefect of Judea.

Daniel: Prefect. Not —

Ruth: — not procurator, which is what Tacitus called him. Prefect was his actual title at the time. Which tells you even Tacitus got a detail wrong, and the stone got it right.

Daniel: And Caiaphas.

Ruth: 1990. Construction workers in Jerusalem broke into a family tomb by accident. Inside, a bone box — an ossuary — beautifully carved, inscribed "Joseph son of Caiaphas." Most scholars think that's the high priest from the trial of Jesus.

Daniel: So the people around Jesus keep showing up in the dirt.

Ruth: The cast keeps checking out. And there are a couple of other outside voices, later and messier. A Syrian named Mara bar Serapion, writing to his son sometime after the year 73, asks what the Jews gained by executing their "wise king." He doesn't name him. Most scholars think he means Jesus. And the Babylonian Talmud — hostile Jewish tradition — says a man called Yeshu was hanged on the eve of Passover. That's late, and it's not friendly. But notice: nobody in the ancient world, friend or enemy, ever argues he didn't exist. They argue about who he was.

Daniel: Then where does "he never existed" even come from?

Ruth: Mostly the last couple hundred years. There's a small group today — they call themselves mythicists. A few have real doctorates. Richard Carrier is the best known. But their case depends on reading Paul as if he only believed in a heavenly Jesus, never a man on earth. And then you hit Galatians — "James, the Lord's brother" — and they have to explain that away. The field as a whole just isn't persuaded.

Daniel: I wish I'd had you in that lecture hall.

Ruth: Honey, I wish I'd been there. I'd have eaten that TA for breakfast.

The Bible's Been Changed Too Many Times

Daniel: Okay. Second objection. And this is the one I hear most. "The Bible's been copied and changed so many times, we have no idea what it originally said."

Ruth: Oh, I love this one. Because it's actually backwards too.

Daniel: Everything's backwards today.

Ruth: It's been that kind of week. Okay. We have more than fifty-eight hundred Greek manuscripts of the New Testament. Some are tiny fragments, some are whole Bibles. Plus thousands more in Latin, Syriac, Coptic.

Daniel: How does that compare to other ancient books?

Ruth: Nothing comes close. Homer's Iliad — the most copied book of the ancient Greek world — has somewhere under two thousand manuscripts. And here's the one that gets my students. Remember Tacitus? The passage about Christus? The first six books of his Annals survive in exactly ONE manuscript. Copied in Germany around the year 1000. And the books with the Christ passage? Also basically one manuscript, from the eleventh century.

Daniel: Wait. So the skeptic who quotes Tacitus as a reliable source —

Ruth: — is trusting one medieval copy. And nobody blinks. Meanwhile the New Testament has thousands, and some go back to the second century.

Daniel: Okay, now I want to be careful, because there's a number people throw around. "The New Testament is ninety-nine point five percent accurate."

Ruth: Yeah. I'd retire that number.

Daniel: Really?

Ruth: It gets passed around a lot, and I've never seen it traced to a careful count. And the real picture is actually more impressive, if you tell it straight. When you compare all those manuscripts, you find somewhere around three to four hundred thousand differences.

Daniel: Whoa. That sounds terrible.

Ruth: It SOUNDS terrible. That's why skeptics love the number. But the overwhelming majority are spelling. Word order — which barely matters in Greek. A scribe writes "Christ Jesus" instead of "Jesus Christ." Daniel Wallace, who's spent his career photographing these manuscripts, estimates that less than one percent of the variants are both meaningful and have a real chance of being original. And here's the kicker — even Bart Ehrman, the skeptic, has said that the essential Christian beliefs aren't affected by any of them.

Daniel: Any of them?

Ruth: None of the core teachings hang on a disputed reading. Now — in fairness — there ARE two famous passages that probably weren't in the originals. The long ending of Mark, chapter sixteen, verses nine to twenty. And the story of the woman caught in adultery in John eight.

Daniel: "Let him who is without sin cast the first stone."

Ruth: That one. Almost certainly a later addition, though maybe a true story that circulated separately. But here's the thing. Open almost any modern Bible and there's a note right there telling you. Nobody's hiding it.

Daniel: And that actually answers the skeptic's question for us. "How do you know it's been changed?" Well — how do YOU know? The only reason anybody knows about those two passages is because we have so many manuscripts to compare.

Ruth: Exactly. You can't spot the changes without the copies. The evidence that it changed is the same evidence that tells you what it said.

The Disciples Stole the Body

Daniel: Third objection. "The disciples stole the body. That's your empty tomb." And you know what's funny? That's the oldest objection in the book. Literally. It's IN the book. Matthew twenty-eight. The guards at the tomb report what happened, and the chief priests pay them to say — quote — "His disciples came by night and stole him away while we slept." And Matthew adds, this story is still told among the Jews to this day.

Ruth: Which is interesting historically. Because you don't invent a rumor in order to refute it. It tells you the opponents in the first century weren't saying "there was no tomb." They were saying "the tomb was empty because somebody took him."

Daniel: They agreed it was empty.

Ruth: They agreed it was empty. They disagreed about why.

Daniel: So here's the usual answer. The disciples died for their claim. Nobody dies for something they know is a lie. All of them except John were martyred —

Ruth: — okay, I have to stop you there. Gently.

Daniel: Go.

Ruth: That "all except John" line — it's mostly later tradition. Sean McDowell did the most careful study of this, a whole book called "The Fate of the Apostles." He went apostle by apostle and graded the evidence. Some are rock solid. James, the son of Zebedee — Acts twelve says Herod had him killed with the sword, around 44. Peter — very strong evidence he was executed in Rome. Paul, same. And James, Jesus's brother — Josephus, the stoning in 62.

Daniel: And the others?

Ruth: For some, the evidence is thin. Later legends. And in his updated edition, McDowell concluded that at least two of the apostles probably died of natural causes.

Daniel: Okay. So I need to say it more carefully.

Ruth: You do. But here's the thing — the careful version is still strong. Every one of them was willing to suffer. They were beaten, jailed, run out of town. That part is all over our earliest sources. And the ones we can check — Peter, Paul, the two Jameses — died for it.

Daniel: And it's not just that they died for a belief. Lots of people die for a belief.

Ruth: Right, I was going to say —

Daniel: — but those people BELIEVE it. If the disciples stole the body, they wouldn't believe it. They'd KNOW it was a lie. They'd be dying for a story they made up themselves. Over a bag of nothing.

Ruth: That's the part I can't get around, honestly. People die for lies all the time — but lies they've been told. Not lies they told. Liars make terrible martyrs.

Daniel: Now THAT'S going on a t-shirt.

Ruth: And there's something even earlier than the martyrdoms. That passage I saw under glass — First Corinthians fifteen. "Christ died, was buried, was raised, appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve, then to more than five hundred at once." Paul says he's passing on something he received. Most scholars — skeptics included — date that formula to within a few years of the crucifixion.

Daniel: A few YEARS.

Ruth: That's not a legend growing over centuries. That's the news.

Daniel: So what do skeptical scholars do with it?

Ruth: The serious alternative isn't theft anymore. It's visions. A German scholar, Gerd Lüdemann, argued that Peter, in grief and guilt, had a vivid hallucination of Jesus, and it spread.

Daniel: Okay, but — — hallucinations are private. Like dreams. Five hundred people don't have the same dream on the same afternoon.

Ruth: That's the standard reply, yes.

Daniel: And look at who's on that list. James. Jesus's own brother. In the gospels, his family thinks he's lost his mind. Mark three. James doesn't believe in him. And then, after the resurrection, James is leading the church in Jerusalem, and dies for it in 62. What turns a skeptical brother into a martyr?

Ruth: And Paul.

Daniel: And Paul! He's not a grieving friend. He's hunting Christians down. He's the last guy on earth who's going to have a hopeful vision of Jesus.

Ruth: I'll grant you, the persecutor and the skeptical brother are the two hardest pieces for the hallucination theory. Lüdemann's answer was basically "guilt," for both. I've never found that very satisfying.

He Was Just a Good Moral Teacher

Daniel: Number four. This is the polite one. "Jesus was a great moral teacher. I just don't think he was God."

Ruth: That's the one my colleagues say at dinner parties.

Daniel: And C.S. Lewis took it apart in 1952, in "Mere Christianity." He said a man who said the things Jesus said wouldn't be a great moral teacher. He'd either be a lunatic — on the level of a man who says he's a poached egg — or something worse. Or he's exactly who he said he was. Liar, lunatic, or Lord.

Ruth: "On the level of a poached egg." I always forget that's actually in there.

Daniel: It's in there! And look at what he claimed. Mark two. A paralyzed man is lowered through a roof, and Jesus says, "Son, your sins are forgiven." And the teachers of the law are sitting there thinking — who can forgive sins but God alone? And they're RIGHT. If I step on your foot, I can forgive you. I can't forgive the guy who stepped on YOUR foot.

Ruth: That's a good way to put it.

Daniel: John ten: "I and the Father are one." They pick up stones. John twenty — Thomas sees him and says, "My Lord and my God." And Jesus doesn't correct him. A good teacher would've said, whoa, whoa, sit down, Thomas —

Ruth: — okay, now let me give you the historian's pushback. Because there's a fourth option Lewis kind of skips. Legend. A skeptic would say: Jesus was a Jewish teacher, a prophet maybe, and decades later his followers put those big claims in his mouth. John's gospel especially is late, maybe the 90s.

Daniel: That's the strongest objection, isn't it?

Ruth: It's the serious one. It's the one Ehrman would make.

Daniel: So here's my answer. Mark is the earliest gospel. Most scholars put it around the year 70. And the forgiving-sins scene is in Mark. But go earlier. Philippians two. Paul, writing in the 50s, quotes what looks like an even earlier hymn: Christ Jesus, "who, being in very nature God, did not consider equality with God something to be used to his own advantage." That's twenty-odd years after the cross. Maybe less.

Ruth: You're right that the dating is early. A scholar named Larry Hurtado spent his career on exactly this. His conclusion was that devotion to Jesus as divine — worship — shows up astonishingly early, among Jewish followers, in the first couple of decades. Not as a slow evolution under Greek influence. More like an explosion.

Daniel: An explosion. Among Jews. Who would rather die than worship a man.

Ruth: That's what makes it so historically strange. I'll give you that. It's one of the strangest things in the ancient world.

Okay, but let's be honest

Daniel: Last one. And it's not really a history question. "A good God wouldn't allow suffering."

Ruth: That's the one that actually keeps people up at night.

Daniel: It is. And I want to answer it with a picture instead of an argument. Christianity is the only faith I know of where God himself has scars. He didn't watch suffering from far away. He walked into it. Isaiah fifty-three: "he was pierced for our transgressions... by his wounds we are healed." Philippians two: he humbled himself to death, "even death on a cross." And Revelation five — John looks at the throne of heaven, the center of the universe, and what does he see? "A Lamb, looking as if it had been slain." In heaven. Still with the wounds.

Ruth: Okay. Now I'm going to be the historian of religion, because I'd be doing you a disservice if I let "the only God with scars" go by without a footnote.

Daniel: I figured.

Ruth: Old Norse poetry. The Hávamál. Odin hangs himself on the world tree for nine nights, wounded with a spear. And there are other gods in the ancient world who suffer, or die. So "no other religion has a god who suffered" — as a flat historical claim, a sharp skeptic will knock that down in about thirty seconds.

Daniel: That's fair. Let me sharpen it, then. What does Odin say about why he does it?

Ruth: You've read it.

Daniel: I read it last night, preparing for you. He says he was "given to Odin, myself to myself." He hangs there to win the runes — secret wisdom. Power. For himself.

Ruth: That's — yes. That's the text.

Daniel: And Paul says, Romans five: "God demonstrates his own love for us in this: while we were still sinners, Christ died for us." Not for wisdom. Not for himself. For his enemies. So maybe the honest claim isn't "the only God who suffered." It's "the only God who suffered FOR the people who put him there."

Ruth: I'll — yeah. I'll let that one stand. I can't think of a parallel.

Daniel: And can I give you the other honest part? Because you're about to say it.

Ruth: Go ahead, steal my line.

Daniel: A historian can take you to the cross. Jesus existed, he was crucified under Pilate, the tomb was reported empty, his followers sincerely believed they saw him alive, and they'd rather die than deny it. But the last step — "and he really rose" — a historian can't stamp that.

Ruth: Right. My tools stop at the edge of the tomb. History can tell you what people saw and believed. It can't certify a miracle. That's where you have to answer Jesus's question for yourself. Who do YOU say I am.

Daniel: But the historian also can't make the empty tomb and those appearances go away. They still need an explanation.

Ruth: They do. And every naturalistic explanation I know of has holes in it. I'll say that much on the record.

A Listener Asks

Daniel: We got a message I want to read, because it's the whole reason we did this episode. A listener writes: "My coworker keeps saying Jesus never existed and the Bible's been changed a thousand times. I never know what to say, and when I try, I sound like a jerk."

Ruth: Oh, I've been the coworker. And I've been the jerk.

Daniel: What would you tell them?

Ruth: As a teacher? Ask questions before you make claims. "Which historians say that?" "How would we know what changed?" Most people repeating "Jesus never existed" have never read a single historian on it. A good question does more than a good speech.

Daniel: And Peter says the same thing, first Peter three. "Always be prepared to give an answer to everyone who asks you to give the reason for the hope that you have." And then the part everybody skips: "But do this with gentleness and respect."

Ruth: Gentleness and respect. That's the part that would've worked on me.

Daniel: And Jude says "contend for the faith." Contend. Not "win the argument." Contend. Stand there, with a kind face, and don't move.

Ruth: Like a deacon in Savannah.

Daniel: Exactly like that.

What Do We Do with a Story Like This

Daniel: Let me try pulling it together, and you tell me where I'm overreaching.

Ruth: I've been doing that all episode.

Daniel: You have. Okay. One: Jesus existed. Not a serious question among historians, Christian or not. Tacitus, who hated Christians, and Josephus both mention him, and Paul personally knew his brother. Two: the New Testament is the best-preserved book of the ancient world. Thousands of manuscripts, the differences are overwhelmingly trivial, and the couple of big ones are printed right in your Bible's footnotes. Three: even his enemies agreed the tomb was empty, and the men who claimed to see him alive would have known if it was a lie, and some of them died rather than say so. Four: a merely good teacher doesn't forgive other people's sins — and his followers were worshipping him within a few years. And five: in the face of suffering, Christianity doesn't offer a theory. It offers a God with scars, who got them for his enemies.

Ruth: I'd only add one refinement, and you've already said it. History brings you right up to the tomb. It hands you the evidence. And then it steps back, and the question that's left is the one Jesus asked. Not "who do people say I am." "Who do YOU say I am."

Daniel: Ruth, thank you. This was — this was a gift.

Ruth: Thank you, Daniel. And thank your mother for the scar story. I'm going to be thinking about that one for a while.

Daniel: I'll tell her. To everyone listening — and especially to the nineteen-year-old in the lecture hall who didn't know what to say: you're not standing on nothing. You're standing on more evidence than almost anything else in the ancient world. Now go answer the question. Gently. This has been Dust & Starlight.
