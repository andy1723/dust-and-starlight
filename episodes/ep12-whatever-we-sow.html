What are we teaching artificial intelligence?

AI systems learn whatever they are rewarded for: in 2016 a game-playing AI scored higher by circling a lagoon on fire than by finishing the race. The episode asks what values we are sowing into the machines we build, beside Galatians 6:7, "whatever a man sows, he will reap."

From Episode 12: Whatever We Sow. Host Daniel Voss with Dr. Hannah Lindqvist, computer scientist (machine learning and AI alignment).

The Tournament

Hannah: Can I give you my favorite piece of evidence, though? Because it's from computer science, and it's — honestly, it's beautiful.

Daniel: Please.

Hannah: 1980. A political scientist at the University of Michigan named Robert Axelrod runs a tournament. Not people — computer programs. He invites game theorists to submit strategies for a game called the Prisoner's Dilemma.

Daniel: Remind me how that works.

Hannah: Two players. Each turn, you either cooperate or betray. If you both cooperate, you both do pretty well. If you betray someone who cooperated, you do GREAT and they get nothing. If you both betray, you both do badly. So in any single round, betrayal is tempting. But Axelrod's programs didn't play once. They played two hundred rounds against each other. Over and over. Every program against every other program.

Daniel: So it's a long game. A road, not a step.

Hannah: Exactly. Fourteen strategies entered. Some were very clever. Some were sneaky, built to betray at just the right moment. And the winner was the simplest program in the whole tournament. Submitted by a mathematician named Anatol Rapoport. It's called Tit for Tat.

Daniel: What does it do?

Hannah: It cooperates on the first move. Always. And after that, it just does whatever you did last time. You cooperate, it cooperates. You betray, it betrays once. You come back, it forgives immediately.

Daniel: Huh.

Hannah: And here's the part that gets me. Axelrod ran it again, a second tournament, sixty-three programs this time. Everybody KNEW Tit for Tat had won. They designed programs specifically to beat it. It won again.

Daniel: Whoa.

Hannah: And Tit for Tat never beat a single opponent head to head. Never. Mathematically, it can't. The most it can do in any one matchup is tie. It wins the tournament because it pulls everyone around it into cooperating. The nasty programs win their little battles and lose the war, because they keep dragging each other down.

Daniel: "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth." Hannah, it never beat anybody, and it inherited the tournament.

Hannah: I knew you'd say that.

Daniel: How could I not say that?

Hannah: Axelrod summed up what the winners had in common. Four things. They were nice — never the first to betray. They were retaliatory — they didn't let themselves be walked on. They were forgiving. And they were clear — easy to read, so others could learn to trust them.

Daniel: Okay, now I have to be honest about my own side here. Tit for Tat isn't exactly turning the other cheek. It hits back.

Hannah: It does. But here's the follow-up, and this is where you might win it back. In 1992, Martin Nowak and Karl Sigmund looked at what happens when the world is noisy — when sometimes a player MEANS to cooperate and it comes across as a betrayal. A misunderstanding. Tit for Tat falls apart in that world. Two Tit for Tats can get locked in a feud forever over one accident.

Daniel: Which is every family I know.

Hannah: And what beats it is something called Generous Tit for Tat. It forgives some betrayals without retaliating at all. Just lets them go.

Daniel: Peter asks Jesus, how many times do I forgive my brother? Seven times? And Jesus says — not seven. Seventy times seven. In a noisy world, the forgiving strategy wins.

Hannah: In a noisy world, among players who keep meeting each other, more forgiveness than you'd expect turns out to be the winning move. Nowak went on to write a whole paper in Science in 2006, "Five Rules for the Evolution of Cooperation." The math is real.

Daniel: Paul says it in one line. Romans twelve: "Don't be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good."

What Do We Do with a Story Like This

Daniel: Let me try pulling it together, and you tell me where I'm overreaching.
