Dust and Starlight · Episode 3

Did the Bible say the earth floats in space?

Job 26:7 says God "hangs the earth on nothing," when many ancient cultures pictured the world resting on pillars, animals or solid domes. The episode traces how astronomy came to see an earth suspended in space.

From Episode 3: Hung on Nothing. Host Daniel Voss with Dr. Amara Okonkwo, historian of ancient science and astronomy.

Segment One: the World Needs Something to Stand on

Daniel: Okay, give me the actual lineup. Who believed what?

Amara: Sure. In parts of Hindu cosmology, the earth rests on the backs of four elephants, and the elephants stand on a giant turtle, and the turtle floats on a cosmic serpent coiled in the ocean.

Daniel: So even that system eventually just... floats on something.

Amara: Eventually, yeah — the serpent's usually resting in a boundless ocean, which is its own kind of "it's turtles all the way down."

Daniel: What about the Greeks?

Amara: The Greeks have Atlas — a Titan, physically holding up the sky on his shoulders, as a punishment.

Daniel: So the sky needs a guy.

Amara: The sky needs a guy. And in Egypt, you get something similar but inverted — the sky is the goddess Nut, arched overhead, and the god Shu stands below her, physically holding her up so she doesn't collapse onto the earth.

Daniel: Huh. So across totally unconnected civilizations — Egypt, Greece, India — everyone independently lands on some version of "there has to be a support structure."

Amara: Because that's just how physical intuition works. Nothing in ordinary human experience floats unsupported. Drop something, it falls. So the assumption "the world must be resting on something" isn't dumb — it's actually the more reasonable guess, given what people could observe with their own eyes.

Daniel: And what about the world Job's writer actually lived in? The Ancient Near East specifically?

Amara: Pillars, mostly. Mesopotamian texts talk about the heavens and the earth being held up by literal pillars — sometimes cosmic mountains, sometimes actual described support-structures. Babylonian literature praises the sun god Shamash as the one who suspends the "circle of the lands" from the heavens — so even the sky itself needs something holding it in place from above.

Daniel: So this is the actual cultural water this author was swimming in. Not some distant myth from a continent away — the neighbors, basically.

Amara: Right next door, culturally speaking. Which makes it even more notable if this author is choosing a different image at all. And this shows up in the Hebrew Bible too, in other places — Job himself, earlier in the same book, mentions the pillars of the earth trembling.

Segment Two: the Line in Job

Daniel: Okay, so set the scene for me. What's actually happening in the Book of Job when this line shows up?

Amara: Job has lost basically everything — his wealth, his children, his health — and for chapters, his friends are trying to explain why, mostly by insisting he must have secretly deserved it. Job pushes back, hard. And in chapter twenty-six, he basically says — you think you understand God's power? You don't even understand a fraction of it. And then he starts listing examples of that power, working through creation itself.

Daniel: And that's where the line lands.

Amara: Right in the middle of it — "He stretches out the north over empty space; he hangs the earth on nothing."

Daniel: Say that again, slower. I want to actually sit with it.

Amara: "He hangs the earth on nothing."

Daniel: No pillars in that sentence. No turtle. No Atlas holding anything up. Just — nothing, and the earth is still there.

Amara: Which, in a world where literally every neighboring culture is reaching for some kind of physical support structure, is a strange thing to write down.

Daniel: There's actually a word I want to ask you about — "empty space." Is that a fair translation, or is something getting smoothed over there?

Amara: Good catch, actually. The Hebrew word underneath "empty space" is tohu — and that's not a random word. It's the exact same word used in Genesis chapter one, verse two, describing the earth right at the start of creation, before anything's been formed — usually translated "formless" or "void."

Daniel: So Job is using the creation-story word.

Amara: Right, which most scholars think is deliberate — it ties this line back to the same primordial emptiness Genesis opens with. It's less "outer space" in the modern astronomical sense, and more "the same raw formlessness God worked with at the very beginning," now holding the whole earth up without needing to be filled in with anything solid.

Daniel: Huh. That's a genuinely different read than "ancient author accidentally described a vacuum." That's more like — creation itself is still resting on the same kind of nothing it started from.

Amara: That's a more textually careful way to put it, yes. Whether or not it maps onto modern physics, it's doing real theological work inside its own text — connecting this moment back to the very beginning of everything.

Daniel: Do we know if the author meant that literally? Like — astronomically, scientifically literally?

Amara: Honestly, we can't fully know that. It's poetry, and ancient poetry doesn't always draw the line between literal and figurative the way we'd want it to. But even read as poetry, it's making a specific claim — the earth isn't propped up by anything visible. It's suspended. That's the image, whether or not the author had any concept of gravity behind it.

Segment Three: Same Idea, Totally Different Room

Daniel: Okay, here's the part that actually surprised me when I read into this. This idea doesn't just show up in Job.

Amara: No — it shows up, completely independently, in Greek philosophy. There's a thinker named Anaximander, from the city of Miletus, roughly sixth century BCE.

Daniel: And what does he argue?

Amara: That the earth doesn't rest on anything. No water, no pillar, nothing underneath it. His actual reasoning is almost mathematical — he says the earth stays where it is because it's equally distant from everything around it in every direction, so it has no reason to move toward any one side rather than another.

Daniel: Huh. So it's not even a religious claim for him. It's basically a symmetry argument.

Amara: Completely secular reasoning. No gods holding anything up, no myth. Just — logically, if it's equidistant from everything, why would it fall in any particular direction?

Daniel: That's wild. That's a genuinely modern way to think about it. Like — that's not far from how a physicist would frame a stability problem today.

Amara: It's remarkably close, yeah. Historians of science often point to Anaximander specifically because of that — reasoning his way to an unsupported earth using pure logic, roughly two thousand years before Newton actually explains the force that makes it work.

Daniel: Okay, so — devil's advocate. Doesn't this kind of undercut the "wow, the Bible knew something special" angle? If a Greek philosopher gets to basically the same place using nothing but logic, maybe "the earth doesn't need a physical support" isn't such an exotic insight after all.

Amara: That's a completely fair challenge, and I don't think you can wave it away. It suggests this idea was, in some sense, reachable — by careful observation and reasoning, without any special revelation required.

Daniel: So it's less "impossible to arrive at" and more "almost nobody bothered to actually push the logic that far."

Amara: That might be the more honest way to frame it, yeah. Most cultures had the observational tools to ask the question. Very few actually followed it all the way to "and therefore, nothing is holding it up."

Daniel: I actually think that's a more interesting claim than the flashier version, if I'm honest.

Amara: I'd agree with you there.

Daniel: Does the idea stick, though? Or does it get abandoned after Anaximander?

Amara: It gets picked up and reworked, actually — a couple centuries later, Aristotle tackles the same question from a completely different angle. He doesn't use Anaximander's symmetry argument. Instead, he argues every element has a "natural place" it moves toward — fire and air rise, earth and water sink toward the center of the universe.

Daniel: So earth's natural place is the center.

Amara: Right, and since the earth as a whole is already at the center, it has nowhere left to fall. It's not hanging on nothing because nothing's pulling it — in Aristotle's system, it's already sitting exactly where its nature wants it to be. No support needed, because there's no "down" left to fall toward.

Daniel: That's a totally different mechanism than Anaximander's symmetry argument, but it lands in the same place — earth, unsupported, just sitting there.

Amara: Which is part of why this idea has such long legs. You get at least three completely different reasoning paths inside Greek philosophy alone, before you even get to Newton actually supplying the force that makes it work.

Daniel: Huh. So it's not really one lucky guess lining up with one ancient poem. It's more like — this conclusion kept getting rediscovered, independently, by whoever actually sat down and thought hard about it.

Amara: That's a fair way to characterize it, yeah.

Segment Four: Okay, but: Let's Be Honest About This

Daniel: Alright, you told me to hold a thought a few minutes ago. The pillars.

Amara: Right, I did say we'd get there.

Daniel: Because if I remember right, Job — the same book — talks about pillars of the earth trembling somewhere else.

Amara: Chapter nine, yes. And it's not the only place — Hannah's prayer in the First Book of Samuel says God has "set the world upon" the pillars of the earth. So within Scripture itself, you've got both images — the earth on pillars, and the earth hanging on nothing.

Daniel: Wait — so that's not even internally consistent?

Amara: Not if you're reading it as a technical cosmology textbook, no. If you try to force all of it into one coherent physical model, you get a contradiction pretty fast.

Daniel: Okay, so how do you square that? Because that feels like a real problem for the "this was a precise scientific claim" reading.

Amara: I think the honest answer is that ancient Hebrew poetry moves fluidly between different images to make different points, without necessarily committing to one fixed, literal cosmology across the whole text. "Pillars trembling" is describing catastrophe, upheaval — it's borrowing a common image of the world shaking on its foundations. "Hung on nothing" is describing something else — vastness, God's power operating without any visible mechanism at all. They're doing different rhetorical jobs.

Daniel: I hear that, but I want to push on it a little more, because it feels almost too convenient — like, whichever verse supports the point you're making in the moment, that's the "real" cosmology, and the other one's just poetry.

Amara: That's a fair suspicion, honestly, and I'd be lying if I said scholars have this fully settled. Some do think you're reading too much modern science into "hung on nothing," precisely because of passages like the pillars one — that it's not meant to be a uniquely advanced statement at all, just one image among several, and we're the ones cherry-picking the one that sounds impressive to modern ears.

Daniel: Okay. And then there's the dating question, right? Because earlier you said Anaximander's around the sixth century BCE.

Amara: Right, and this is the part I think people skip past too quickly. Nobody actually knows when the Book of Job was written. Some traditional views place it extremely early — patriarchal era, close to two thousand BCE. But a lot of modern scholarship places the actual composition much later — anywhere from around the seventh century down to the fourth century BCE.

Daniel: Wait. So it's possible Job was written around the same time as Anaximander. Or even after him.

Amara: It's genuinely possible. We don't have a firm enough date to say Job clearly came first. Anyone telling you with total confidence which one came first is overstating what we actually know.

Daniel: Huh. Okay, that's — I appreciate you saying that directly instead of just letting the "ancient text beat the Greeks to it" version stand.

Amara: It'd be an easy thing to gloss over on a show built around these connections. I don't think it's honest to.

Segment Five: What Was Actually Holding It Up

Daniel: Okay, so both of these ancient sources land on "nothing is holding it up" — one through poetry, one through logic. Neither one actually explains why that works, though, right?

Amara: Right, and that's an important gap. Saying "it's not resting on anything" isn't the same as explaining what keeps it from just falling. That question sits open for a very long time.

Daniel: So who actually answers it?

Amara: Isaac Newton, 1687, in the Principia. He formulates the law of universal gravitation — every object with mass pulls on every other object with mass, and the strength of that pull depends on how much mass and how far apart they are.

Daniel: So the earth isn't resting on nothing in the sense of just floating there with no explanation. It's being held in place by an actual force.

Amara: Exactly — gravity, from the sun mostly, keeps it in orbit, and gravity is also why you don't need a floor under your feet on a spinning planet. It's not that nothing is happening. It's that the "something" holding it in place is an invisible force, not a physical object.

Daniel: Huh — so "hung on nothing" turns out to be almost the perfect phrase for it. Not "hung on emptiness, full stop." Hung on an invisible force that doesn't look like anything.

Amara: That's a nice way to put it, actually. Nothing you can see. Nothing you can touch. But absolutely something, physically speaking.

Daniel: And Newton doesn't know about that Job line, presumably.

Amara: He very likely did, actually — Newton read Scripture extensively and wrote enormous amounts of theological commentary alongside his physics. But he wasn't using Job as a scientific source. He got there through mathematics and observation of planetary motion, completely independent of the text.

Daniel: Another totally separate path landing on the same idea.

Amara: Three, if you count Anaximander. Poetry, pure logic, and then finally rigorous mathematical physics — three completely different methods, all converging on "nothing physical is holding this up."

Segment Six: Seeing It for Real

Daniel: Okay, so when does someone actually get to just... look and confirm it?

Amara: The most famous moment is probably 1968 — Apollo 8, the first crewed mission to orbit the moon. The astronauts turn a camera back toward Earth and capture the photograph that gets called "Earthrise" — the whole planet, hanging in black space over the lunar horizon.

Daniel: No pillars. No turtle.

Amara: Just a blue sphere, floating in total darkness, with visibly nothing under it or around it.

Daniel: And it just kept going from there, right? More photos, more confirmation.

Amara: Right — 1972, the "Blue Marble" photo from Apollo 17, probably the most reproduced photograph in history. Then in 1990, Voyager 1 turns around from nearly four billion miles away and takes what gets called the "Pale Blue Dot" — Earth as a single point of light, suspended in the vastness, genuinely hanging on nothing as far as the eye could ever tell.

Daniel: Whoa. And now it's just... constant. Right now, today, there are cameras on the International Space Station livestreaming exactly that.

Amara: Right now, as we're recording this, somebody could pull up a live feed and just watch the earth turning in space with nothing under it. It went from a poetic claim, to a philosophical argument, to a mathematical law, to something you can just casually check on your phone.

Daniel: Does actually seeing it do something different to people than just knowing it intellectually? Like — is there a difference between "I understand gravity" and "I have watched the earth hang there with my own eyes"?

Amara: There's actually a documented name for this. Psychologists and astronauts call it the "overview effect" — this well-reported shift in perspective that happens specifically when someone sees the whole earth from space, hanging there, borderless, with nothing visibly holding it up. Multiple astronauts have described it as a genuinely disorienting, almost overwhelming experience — not just "oh, interesting," but something closer to awe, or grief, or a total recalibration of scale.

Daniel: Huh — so the "hung on nothing" image isn't just accurate. Actually seeing it apparently does something to a person that just believing it doesn't.

Amara: That's the pattern, at least as it's been reported. Which is a strange echo of the Job passage, honestly — the whole point of that speech is that Job hasn't personally witnessed God's power directly, he's being asked to trust it anyway. And then, thousands of years later, astronauts get to actually see the thing the poem was gesturing at, and by most accounts it hits them just as hard, maybe harder, than the poetry ever could.

Segment Seven: What Do We Do with a Story Like This

Daniel: Okay, last one. Given everything — the pillars problem, the dating uncertainty with Anaximander, all of it — where do you actually land on this?

Amara: Honestly? I keep coming back to the posture of the line, more than its precision. "He hangs the earth on nothing" isn't really trying to be a technical claim. It's an argument about trust — about something enormous being held up without any visible means of support, and staying held up anyway.

Daniel: Which is a strange thing to say about a planet, but it's also kind of a strange thing to ask of a person.

Amara: That's exactly the parallel I keep landing on. The whole speech in Job is about trusting a power you can't fully see or verify — spoken by a man who has lost almost everything and still isn't sure why. And then, right in the middle of it, this offhand image of the entire world hanging, unsupported, on nothing but that same invisible power.

Daniel: So the "hanging on nothing" isn't just describing the earth. It's describing what it feels like to trust something you can't put your hand under and check.

Amara: I think that's really the heart of it. And what I find genuinely moving is that centuries later, physics hands us almost the same image back, just literalized — gravity really is invisible, really is untouchable, and really is what's holding everything in place regardless of whether anyone can see it working.

Daniel: Faith and physics ending up in the same spot, even if they got there by completely different roads.

Amara: Different roads, same destination — an unsupported world, held up by something real that nobody can see. I don't think that resolves into a tidy scientific victory lap. I think it's just genuinely worth sitting with.

Daniel: Can I try to pull the whole episode together, and you tell me if I'm overreaching?

Amara: Go for it.

Daniel: We've got a poet who says the earth hangs on nothing, using the same word for "nothing" that shows up at the very start of creation. A philosopher a few centuries later, maybe before him, maybe after, arguing the exact same conclusion from pure symmetry, no gods involved at all. Then Aristotle, landing there again from a completely different theory. Then Newton, finally explaining the actual force behind it, while also writing pages of theology on the side. And then, twentieth century, actual human beings leaving the planet and looking back to see it with their own eyes — and by every account, being shaken by what they saw, the same way Job's speech seems to want its listener to be shaken.

Amara: When you lay it out like that, it's less one lucky sentence and more — the same true thing, apparently, being rediscovered, over and over, by completely different people, using completely different tools.

Daniel: Poetry, logic, physics, and then just... eyesight.

Amara: Four different ways of arriving at the same unsettling, kind of beautiful fact — that the ground under all of us isn't resting on anything you could point to. It never was. And somehow, it's still there.

Daniel: Amara, thank you for this one. This was a good one.

Amara: Anytime, Daniel.

Daniel: That's Dust & Starlight. If this cracked something open for you — or you think we got something wrong — tell us. We'd genuinely rather be corrected than comfortable.
