Is there any evidence for God?

Not proof, but evidence: the universe runs on mathematics so precisely that a pen found Neptune and an equation predicted antimatter, and science can't say why there are laws at all. The episode weighs that against naturalist replies and the hard question of why God seems hidden.

From Episode 19: By Measure and Number. Host Daniel Voss with Dr. Katarina Novak, mathematician and philosopher of mathematics.

The Unreasonable Effectiveness

Katarina: Okay, this is my favorite thing in the world, so stop me if I get carried away.

Daniel: I won't.

Katarina: In 1960, the physicist Eugene Wigner — he won a Nobel Prize a few years later — wrote an essay with a wonderful title. "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences."

Daniel: Unreasonable.

Katarina: Unreasonable! Because here's the puzzle. Mathematicians often invent things for no practical reason at all. Just because they're beautiful. Just — playing. And then, decades later, physicists discover that nature is built exactly like that.

Daniel: Give me an example.

Katarina: In 1928, Paul Dirac writes down an equation to describe the electron. Beautiful equation. But it has a strange extra solution. It seems to say there should be another particle — like an electron, but with the opposite charge. Nobody had ever seen one.

Daniel: And —

Katarina: — and four years later, 1932, Carl Anderson finds it. In cosmic rays. The positron. Antimatter. Because an equation said it should be there.

Daniel: Like Neptune.

Katarina: Like Neptune. Or James Clerk Maxwell. In the 1860s, his equations for electricity and magnetism predict invisible waves that travel at the speed of light. Twenty years later, Heinrich Hertz makes them in a lab. Radio waves. Every phone call you've ever made started in Maxwell's notebook.

Daniel: Okay, that one — wow.

Katarina: Or the Higgs boson. Predicted on paper in 1964. Found in 2012. Forty-eight years of waiting for nature to — to keep the appointment.

Daniel: Okay, but those are all physicists writing equations FOR physics. You said mathematicians invent things for fun —

Katarina: — and then nature uses them. Yes. My favorite is Bernhard Riemann. 1854. He gives a lecture on geometry where the usual rules break. Parallel lines that meet. Space that — curves. Pure imagination. Nobody thought it described anything real.

Daniel: And —

Katarina: — sixty years later, Einstein needs a way to describe gravity, and he finds Riemann's geometry sitting on the shelf. Waiting for him. General relativity is written in it. The shape of space and time was a mathematician's daydream first.

Daniel: That's — that's honestly eerie.

Katarina: It gets eerier. "Imaginary numbers." The square root of minus one. Italian mathematicians in the 1500s used them as a trick to solve equations and — honestly — were embarrassed by them. They called them useless. Fictitious.

Daniel: Let me guess. Not useless.

Katarina: The basic equation of quantum mechanics has the square root of minus one sitting right in the middle of it. The "imaginary" numbers turned out to be how atoms actually work. Every transistor in your phone depends on them.

Daniel: Wigner had a line about this, didn't he?

Katarina: He did, and I have it on my office wall. "The miracle of the appropriateness of the language of mathematics for the formulation of the laws of physics is a wonderful gift which we neither understand nor deserve."

Daniel: "A gift." That's — a very interesting word for a physicist to use.

Katarina: I've always thought so. And Einstein, in 1936: "The eternally incomprehensible thing about the world is its comprehensibility."

Daniel: So the mystery isn't that the universe is complicated.

Katarina: The mystery is that it's UNDERSTANDABLE. It didn't have to be. It could have been — chaos. Noise. Or orderly, but in a way no human mind could ever follow.

What Do We Do with a Story Like This

Daniel: Let me try pulling it together. Tell me where I'm overreaching.

Katarina: With pleasure.

Daniel: "Zero evidence" confuses evidence with proof. Evidence is weight on a scale. And one heavy weight is that the universe runs on mathematics — math so precise that a pen found Neptune, an equation found antimatter, and a notebook gave us radio. Wigner called it a gift we don't understand. Einstein called comprehensibility the eternal mystery. The Bible said it first: measuring lines, fixed laws, measure and number and weight, and "in the beginning was the Logos." Science can't answer why there's something rather than nothing without assuming laws. And this isn't a God of the gaps. It grows as science grows.

Katarina: My refinements. One — none of this is proof. A naturalist can say "the universe is mathematical, full stop, no reason needed." Some physicists, like Max Tegmark, say the universe simply IS math. That's a real option. Two — divine hiddenness is still standing for me. Your answer is serious, but it's not airtight. And three...

Daniel: Three?

Katarina: Three is that I came in thinking "zero evidence" was an overstatement. I'm leaving thinking it's — careless. There's something here. A room with the light on. I still don't know who left it on. But I don't think it's nobody.

Katarina: Don't put it on a mug.
