Dust and Starlight · Episode 1

Did the Bible know something about blood that medicine missed?

Leviticus 17:11 says "the life of the flesh is in the blood." For about two thousand years doctors did the opposite and drained blood to heal people, until bloodletting fell out of favor in the 1800s.

From Episode 1: The Blood Code. Host Daniel Voss with Dr. Elena Reyes, historian of medicine.

Segment Three: the Turn: Harvey and Circulation

Daniel: So when does this actually start to crack?

Elena: 1628 is the real hinge. William Harvey publishes De Motu Cordis — "On the Motion of the Heart."

Daniel: And what does he actually do differently? Because presumably people had looked at hearts before.

Elena: Sure, but Harvey does something nobody had really done rigorously — he quantifies it. He calculates: if the heart pumps a certain volume every beat, times a certain number of beats per hour...

Daniel: Uhm... you'd need an impossible amount of blood being made and destroyed every day.

Elena: Exactly — unless it's not being endlessly produced and consumed at all. Unless it's a fixed volume, moving in a loop, over and over.

Daniel: So it's the same blood. Just going around.

Elena: Just going around. And that's the moment blood stops being "a humor you might have too much of" and starts being "a closed, finite, precious delivery system for the whole body."

Elena: You'd think so.

Elena: I mean there's an entire economy built around it. Barber-surgeon guilds, bleeding manuals, hospital wards organized around it. Disproving the theory isn't enough — you need overwhelming evidence of harm before institutions actually change what they're doing with their hands.

Elena: It really is. But — and this is the wild part — the boy survives. He even seems to improve.

Elena: It seems to work. Denis does it again on another patient. Also seems fine. He's emboldened. And then he transfuses a third patient — a man named Antoine Mauroy — multiple times, and Mauroy dies.

Elena: Denis gets put on trial. His widow accuses him of murder.

Elena: Actually — no. It came out that Mauroy's wife had been poisoning him with arsenic the whole time. Denis is cleared of the murder charge specifically.

Elena: It's genuinely one of the stranger footnotes in the history of medicine.

Elena: Essentially, yes — because the scandal itself was enough. Both the French and English authorities move to shut the whole practice down. Transfusion just gets banned. Not for a decade. For close to two centuries.

Elena: That's the thing about scandal versus science — it doesn't always follow the actual verdict.

Daniel: So what actually breaks the logjam? Because clearly something does, eventually.

Elena: The real answer to why some of those early transfusions worked and others killed people wasn't found until 1901 — an Austrian scientist named Karl Landsteiner figures out that human blood actually comes in different types. A, B, O, and later AB. Mix the wrong ones together and the blood cells clump — agglutinate — and that's what was silently killing some of Denis's patients, and some of the transfusion attempts for the next TWO hundred years after him.

Daniel: So this whole time, nobody had actually done anything wrong technique-wise, necessarily — they just didn't know blood wasn't all... interchangeable.

Elena: Exactly. It's not that the idea of transfusion was bad. It's that they were missing one piece of information that made the difference between saving a life and ending one, and they had no way to see it.

Elena: 1930. And it's really the moment transfusion goes from "terrifying gamble" to "routine, life-saving procedure." Which, again — plays right back into that same one-line claim. The life is in the blood. Turns out it's specific enough that you can't even mix two different people's without checking first.
