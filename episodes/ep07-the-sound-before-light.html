Did the universe ring with sound before light could travel?

For its first 380,000 years the universe was a hot, opaque plasma ringing with sound waves, and their imprint is still visible in the cosmic microwave background. Genesis 1 puts God's word before "let there be light," and the episode asks what to make of that order.

What Physicists Actually Mean by Sound

Ingrid: When I say "sound" in the early universe, let go of air molecules hitting an eardrum. There's no ear, there's no air. What there IS, for the first few hundred thousand years, is an incredibly hot, dense soup — mostly photons and charged particles, protons and electrons, all locked together.

Daniel: Locked together how?

Ingrid: The electrons are free — not bound into atoms yet, too hot — so light can't travel far before it smacks into one. That's Thomson scattering. A photon in that plasma goes practically nowhere before it's absorbed or knocked sideways.

Daniel: So it's — foggy. Opaque.

Ingrid: Completely opaque. Like being inside a cloud so thick you can't see your own hand. But that same plasma behaves exactly like a compressible fluid. Gravity pulls matter toward denser clumps. Trapped photon pressure pushes back, resisting the compression.

Daniel: Pull, push, pull, push—

Ingrid: —which is literally the physics of a sound wave. Compression and rarefaction, propagating through a medium at a characteristic speed. In this plasma, that speed works out to a bit over half the speed of light.

Daniel: Half the speed of light is a strange speed for "sound."

Ingrid: It's a strange fluid! But the equations don't care — same wave equations you'd use for sound in air, just a wildly different medium and speed. Cosmologists call this whole field "acoustic oscillations." Not sound-LIKE. Acoustic. It's in the technical literature, not just Cramer's sonification.

Daniel: And this starts — when?

Ingrid: The seeds get planted incredibly early — fractions of a second in, from tiny quantum fluctuations stretched out during the universe's fastest expansion. Once a region's fluctuation is large enough, the oscillation begins. So this "sound" is ringing out from close to the very beginning, and it keeps ringing for roughly the next—

How We Actually Know This: A Real History

Daniel: Before we get to what stops the ringing — how do we even know any of this? This isn't something you can go look at.

Ingrid: It's genuinely one of my favorite stories in all of science, because it starts by accident. 1965. Two radio astronomers at Bell Labs in New Jersey — Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson — trying to use a big horn antenna for radio astronomy, keep picking up this faint, steady hiss. Same in every direction. They think it's the equipment.

Daniel: A problem with the hardware.

Ingrid: They clean pigeon droppings out of the antenna. Genuinely. They think it might be that. It wasn't the pigeons.

Ingrid: Here's where it gets almost unbelievable. Forty miles away, at Princeton, a team led by Robert Dicke — with a young theorist named Jim Peebles, plus Peter Roll and David Wilkinson — had independently worked out that a hot Big Bang should have left behind exactly this kind of faint, uniform microwave glow, around ten kelvin. They were actively building a detector to go find it. Before they finished, word got back that two radio astronomers up the road had already stumbled onto an unexplained hiss.

Daniel: They got scooped by an accident.

Ingrid: While actively hunting the exact same thing. Penzias and Wilson didn't know what they had; Dicke's team knew exactly what it would look like if someone found it. Penzias and Wilson got the 1978 Nobel for the discovery. Peebles — that same young theorist — got his own Nobel decades later, in 2019, for the theory built on top of it.

Ingrid: Huge. Stephen Hawking told The Times, April 1992, that the follow-up anisotropy result was — I'm quoting — "the greatest discovery of the century, if not of all time." Smoot's own team, presenting it to a room of physicists, got an audible gasp from the audience.

Regions at maximum compression or rarefaction the instant the ringing stopped show up as peaks. It's a frozen photograph of a sound wave, taken the moment the music cut out.

The Sound That's Still Out There

Daniel: You said "frozen." What actually stops the ringing?

Ingrid: Recombination. Around 380,000 years in, the universe has cooled enough — down to about three thousand kelvin — that electrons finally stick to protons, forming stable, neutral hydrogen. Once most of the free electrons are gone, there's nothing left for photons to keep bouncing off.

Daniel: So the fog just — clears.

Ingrid: Essentially all at once, cosmologically speaking. Photons trapped for 380,000 years suddenly have an open road. That's the light Penzias and Wilson picked up — it's been traveling in a straight line for over thirteen billion years, cooling from something blisteringly hot down to about 2.7 kelvin, which lands it in the microwave part of the spectrum today.

Daniel: And the sound itself — what happens to it at that exact moment?

Ingrid: It freezes. Locked into place, like a photograph. And this is where it gets almost unreasonably useful, because that frozen ringing left a mark on where matter clumped. In 2005, a team led by Daniel Eisenstein, using the Sloan Digital Sky Survey, found that same frozen sound-wave scale — about 490 million light-years across — imprinted in how galaxies are distributed, billions of years later. Cosmologists use it as a ruler for measuring the universe's expansion.

Daniel: So the actual sound from the first few hundred thousand years is still shaping where galaxies sit today.

Ingrid: Still shaping it. This isn't one lucky data point — it's decades of independent confirmation converging on the same story: a universe ringing with real, physical sound, for hundreds of thousands of years, before light was ever free to travel anywhere.

Ingrid: I'd only say — be careful with "required." Nobody's required to draw the line either way. That's a choice you're making, not a conclusion the data hands you.

Daniel: Fair. It's a choice. I'm telling you which one I'm making.

What the Ringing Told Us

Daniel: This isn't just a nice story about an echo, though. You said earlier this is how you actually measure things.

Ingrid: One of the most productive measurements in modern cosmology. How fast a sound wave travels, and how far it gets before freezing, depends on exactly what the universe is made of — how much ordinary matter, how much dark matter, how curved or flat space itself is. When Planck maps those peaks in detail, it's not just confirming the ringing happened. It's a full readout of the universe's ingredient list.

Daniel: And what does that readout say?

Ingrid: Space itself is flat — not curved like a sphere, not saddle-shaped — to within about two-tenths of one percent. Ordinary atomic matter, the stuff we're made of, is only about five percent of everything there is. Roughly twenty-seven percent is dark matter. The remaining sixty-eight percent is dark energy, accelerating the universe's expansion.

Daniel: We got the flatness of the entire universe out of ancient sound waves.

Ingrid: Out of exactly how many oscillation cycles a wave of a given size managed to complete before recombination froze it. That's the measurement. It's staggering how much information is packed into a graph of bumps and dips.

Ingrid: That's an interpretation layered on top of the measurement. The measurement itself doesn't say "designed" or "undesigned" — it just says "flat, and here's the recipe."
