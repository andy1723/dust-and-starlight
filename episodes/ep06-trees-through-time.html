Dust and Starlight · Episode 6

How can fossil trees stand upright through several layers of rock?

Polystrate fossils, trunks buried upright across several layers, show those layers built up quickly, before the wood could rot. The episode weighs geologists' local rapid-burial explanations against a reading tied to the Flood.

Segment Two: the Trees That Shouldn't Be Standing

Daniel: Give me the specific sites. Where are these actually found?

Renata: Joggins, Nova Scotia is the most famous — it's a UNESCO World Heritage site now, actually, partly because of this. Cliffs full of upright fossil trees, cutting through coal seams, sandstone, shale, layer after layer.

Daniel: And Yellowstone too, you said.

Renata: Specimen Ridge, yeah — there's a whole hillside there people call the "fossil forest." Dozens of standing petrified trunks, stacked through many, many layers of volcanic material.

Daniel: And these aren't like — small. We're talking full-size trees.

Renata: Full-size. Root systems intact, flared out at the base like they were actually growing there. Some over a meter across.

Daniel: What about that one you mentioned earlier — the arctic one, up in the islands?

Renata: Axel Heiberg, up in the Canadian Arctic — that one's actually kind of wild, because some of the wood there isn't even fully petrified. It's old enough, by standard dating, to be tens of millions of years, and yet parts of it can still be cut with an axe and burned like ordinary firewood.

Daniel: Wait, that seems like it should be a bigger deal than it sounds like you're making it.

Renata: It's a genuinely interesting preservation puzzle, and I don't want to undersell that. The short version is cold, waterlogged, low-oxygen burial can preserve wood chemically for an extremely long time without full petrification — permafrost and peat bogs do something similar on shorter timescales. It's not proof of a young age on its own, but it is a real reminder that "old" and "looks freshly cut" aren't actually contradictory once you understand the preservation chemistry.

Daniel: Okay, so — the obvious question. Wood rots. Fast, usually. How does a standing tree survive long enough to get buried under, what, dozens of feet of sediment?

Renata: It doesn't, under normal conditions. That's the actual scientific consensus, and it's worth saying plainly — this isn't the creationist claim versus the mainstream claim. Everyone agrees a tree can't stand there for ten thousand years while dirt drifts down on it a grain at a time. If it's still standing, upright, roots intact, the burial has to have been fast.

Daniel: How fast are we actually talking, in real terms? Days? Weeks?

Renata: Depends on the tree and the environment, but generally we're talking weeks to a few years at most for the burial itself, not centuries. And then separately, the wood has to get mineralized — actually petrify — reasonably quickly too, or it'll just decompose underground even once it's covered. That usually needs mineral-rich water moving through it steadily, which is exactly what you get in a lot of these volcanic or swampy, sediment-heavy environments.

Daniel: So it's a two-part problem. Bury it fast, then also chemically preserve it fast.

Renata: Right, and both parts have known, ordinary geological explanations. Neither part requires anything exotic. It's just that both parts together are a lot less common than plain old slow burial, which is why polystrate fossils stand out as unusual rather than being everywhere.

Daniel: So Kent Hovind — I want to bring him in directly here, because a lot of this episode's actually built off a seminar of his — he says exactly that. That the tree had to be buried "in a hurry," his words, and that this proves the whole layer system is wrong.

Renata: The first half of that is just correct, honestly. Derek Ager — who was the president of the British Geological Association, about as mainstream as it gets — said almost the same thing decades ago. Rapid burial, no argument.

Daniel: So where does it start to fall apart, if it falls apart at all?

Renata: The second half. "Therefore the whole layer system is wrong."

Segment Three: Fast Here Doesn't Mean Fast Everywhere

Daniel: Okay walk me through that gap.

Renata: So — take Joggins specifically, since it's the best-studied one. Geologists have actually worked out a pretty specific mechanism there. The whole basin was subsiding — sinking — unusually fast, because of salt deposits underground shifting around beneath it. That kind of local sinking creates exactly the right conditions for sediment to pile up fast, again and again, burying trees quickly, one after another, over a long span of time.

Daniel: So it's not one flood event. It's — a repeating local process.

Renata: Right, and that's the key distinction. A geologically fast burial — days, weeks, maybe a season — happening over and over, spread across a genuinely long stretch of time. Not all the trees buried simultaneously in one week.

Daniel: How would you actually tell the difference, though? Like, from the outside, looking at the cliff?

Renata: That's a completely fair question, and it's the crux of the whole thing. You look at what's between the trees. If it's one flood, you'd expect the layers between each buried tree to look like — mud, all similar, all deposited in the same conditions, laid down practically on top of each other. What you actually find at Joggins is different soil horizons, different plant communities in different layers, coal seams that take a genuinely long time to form chemically even under generous assumptions, and — this is the detail that gets me — root systems from a layer growing down into the layer below it, which only makes sense if that lower layer had already hardened into actual ground first.

Daniel: So the tree's own roots are kind of testifying against the single-event reading.

Renata: That's how I'd put it, yeah.

Daniel: Okay, but I have to push on this — because isn't "we found the mechanism" a little bit after-the-fact? Like, you find the weird thing, then you go looking for a local explanation that saves the old-earth timeline.

Renata: I don't think that's an unfair suspicion to raise, honestly, and I don't want to just wave it off—

Daniel: No, go on—

Renata: — because it's a real pattern worth watching for in any field. But the Joggins mechanism isn't invented for this specific puzzle. Salt tectonics and basin subsidence get studied independently, all over the world, for reasons that have nothing to do with fossil trees — oil and gas exploration cares a lot about exactly this kind of basin. So it's not a custom-built rescue. It's an existing, independently useful geological process that happens to explain this too.

Daniel: How many separate trees are we even talking about at a site like that? Because if it's two or three, I could see coincidence. If it's dozens—

Renata: Joggins alone has documented dozens of these upright trunks across the cliff face, at many different heights in the sequence — not clustered in one spot. Which actually cuts against the single-flood reading a little, if you think about it. If it were one event, you'd expect the trees to cluster at roughly one horizon, not be scattered through the entire thickness of the formation.

Daniel: Whereas if it's a repeating process, spread out—

Renata: — you'd expect exactly what you see. Trees at many different heights, because the subsidence-and-burial cycle just kept happening, over and over, for a long time.

Daniel: That's actually a more specific argument than I expected going in. I figured this was going to be a lot more "we're just not sure."

Renata: Joggins specifically has had a lot of attention because of the UNESCO status — it's one of the better-studied fossil sites on the planet, honestly. Not every polystrate location has had that level of scrutiny.

Renata: Some, yeah — there's genuine published back-and-forth on Specimen Ridge specifically, more than most polystrate sites get. I don't think it's fully settled the way Joggins basically is. Renata: It would be dishonest to pretend that. Some sites are cleaner than others. Yellowstone's the messiest one in tonight's lineup.

Renata: That's a fair way to put it, yeah. Which is a less satisfying answer than either side usually gives you, but it's the honest one.

Segment Five: Okay, but: Let's Be Honest About This

Daniel: Alright, so here's the big-picture question. Even granting Joggins is well explained, and Yellowstone's genuinely contested — does the existence of polystrate fossils at all put any real pressure on the idea that the Earth is old?

Renata: I think the honest answer is: less than the flood-geology framing suggests, but not zero.

Daniel: Unpack the "not zero" part. I wasn't expecting that concession.

Renata: It's a real check on lazy uniformitarianism — the assumption that everything, always, happens slowly and gradually, with nothing dramatic ever. That assumption is just wrong, and polystrate fossils are a genuinely good, concrete piece of evidence that it's wrong. Geology in the last few decades has actually moved substantially toward accepting sudden, violent, localized events as completely normal parts of Earth's history — not exceptions that need apologizing for.

Daniel: Can you give me another example of that shift, outside of trees? Because I want to understand how big a deal this actually was for the field.

Renata: The best one is probably Mount St. Helens, actually — the 1980 eruption. It carved a canyon system in a matter of hours, through layered deposits, that looked, structurally, like the kind of thing textbooks used to say required thousands of years of slow erosion. Geologists had to actually reckon with that, in real time, watching it happen. It's part of why the field takes rapid catastrophic processes so much more seriously now than it did fifty years ago.

Daniel: So Hovind's not wrong that geology used to lean too hard on "everything is slow."

Renata: He's not wrong about that historically, no. Where I think the argument overreaches is treating "local catastrophes are real and common" as equivalent to "therefore it was all one global catastrophe, six thousand years ago, and every rock layer on Earth is a record of that single week."

Daniel: What actually rules that stronger version out, though? Concretely.

Renata: Independent dating methods that don't rely on the layers containing the trees at all. Ice cores with hundreds of thousands of annual layers, individually countable, going back far longer than a young-earth timeline allows. Radiometric dating of the volcanic ash itself at sites like Yellowstone — not the fossils, the ash, using completely different physics than anything to do with sediment rates. Varve counting in old lake beds, layers so fine and so consistent they record actual seasons. Those methods don't depend on assuming slow burial of trees at all, and they independently converge on an old Earth.

Daniel: So the trees alone don't decide it either way. It's the trees plus everything else pointing the same direction.

Renata: That's roughly where I land, yeah. The trees are a real, fair correction to sloppy uniformitarian thinking. They're not, on their own, strong enough to flip the whole age of the Earth.
